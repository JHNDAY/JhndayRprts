Science & Institutional Achievement · mRNA Vaccines, NIH, and Institutional Science Under Fire
The Story Behind the Fauci Show Trial: One of the Most Remarkable Technical Accomplishments of Our Lifetime
John Day23:39–26:10
This is a subject about which I have very strong feelings. Let's go over the history of what happened. It began in 1978 when Katalin Karicó earned her PhD in Hungary and immigrated to the United States, getting a fellowship at UPenn. She had a novel idea that would revolutionize vaccine science, but it was not widely recognized and faced a lot of skepticism. UPenn didn't think she was on the right track, so she got marginalized, living hand-to-mouth in different research labs. Between 1997 and 2005, she teamed with Drew Weissman to overcome the fundamental problem: how to deliver the vaccine to cells without the cells rejecting it. She figured it out. Penn's validation came too late—she was way down in the hierarchy, not much recognition. From 2013 to 2019, she jumped ship from UPenn to BioNTech and finalized the RNA program and brought it to fruition.
John Day26:18–30:15
Let me explain how the vaccine works. The virus comes in a package with spikes on the surface—that's the calling card. The spikes find cells with ACE2 receptors, hook in, and give the virus entry to inject the viral genome. The cell's machinery gets hijacked and starts creating viral components, which are destructive to the cell. Thousands of viral packages form and go on to infect more cells. That's how a modest infection becomes a storm that overwhelms and kills the host. The immune system tries to respond to the spikes, but the virus progresses much faster than the immune system can react. Here's the genius of Karicó: they isolated just the instructions to create the spike protein—the calling card—reducing the RNA description from the full virus to about 10% of its size. They packaged it in lipid nanoparticles, got it into the cell, and had the cell create those spikes in great quantity. But crucially: the lethal aspects of the virus were not recreated, so the cell was not destroyed. The spikes appeared on the cell surface throughout the body wherever the vaccine reached. The immune system saw those spikes and created antibodies that destroy the virus. That's breathtakingly elegant.
John Day35:35–37:30
Let me finish the development story and the role that NIH played. Institutional research is complicated and takes many different passes. With COVID, the spike protein has a particular geometric configuration—lock and key. A molecule has a configuration recognized by another structure, and that's what makes the machine work. When COVID attaches to a cell, that spike has a conformational change, and it's no longer recognizable by the immune system. That was a stumbling block in Karicó's design that had to be overcome. The work for that was done at NIH. Graham and Corbett did it under the direction of Tony Fauci. Fauci managed this wide National Institute of Health for so many years, managing many different problems. In his administrative role, he had to make assessments based on scientific understanding and make bets. Did he make mistakes? Of course. If you're a scientist and you're not making mistakes, you're not really doing science—you're not pushing the frontier.
John Day37:38–37:55
It was clear that Fauci backed something very important. Without the NIH's contribution here, there would have been no vaccine. Without Graham and Corbett's work solving the conformational change problem, the vaccine wouldn't work.
