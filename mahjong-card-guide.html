How to read the card

Every line on the card is a recipe for a 14-tile hand. Tiles are written as numbers and letters, grouped with spaces, with a short note in parentheses and a point value at the end.

Colors mean suits, not specific suits

The card prints its hands in colors. Tiles printed in the same color must come from the same suit. Tiles in different colors must come from different suits. The color itself does not tell you which suit: a hand printed in red does not have to use Craks, and green does not have to mean Bams. This year's card prints in red, green, and black.

RedGreenBlack

So if a hand shows one run of numbers in red and another run in green, you need two different suits, and you choose which suit plays each role.

Reading one line

Here is a made-up sample line (it is not a hand from the card) to show how the notation works:

FF 4444 55 6666 77

Read it in groups: two flowers, a kong of 4s, a pair of 5s, a kong of 6s, and a pair of 7s. That adds up to 2 + 4 + 2 + 4 + 2 = 14 tiles. Every Mah Jongg hand totals 14.

White Dragon is zero

The card carries a note at the top left: the White Dragon (often called "Soap") is used as zero. A 0 on the card is always played with a White Dragon tile. The note also says it may be used with any suit, so a zero does not have to match the suit of the numbers around it. Wherever the card shows a plain D for a dragon, that is a real dragon hand and the printed instructions govern which dragon fits.

Glossary of the phrases in parentheses

Any 1 Suit
Every number tile in the hand comes from a single suit.
Any 2 Suits
The number tiles come from two different suits. Each color on the line stands for one of them.
Any 3 Suits
The number tiles are spread across all three suits, one color per suit.
Pair, Pung, Kong, Quint
2, 3, 4, and 5 identical tiles. Larger groups need jokers once you run out of natural tiles, since the set contains only four of each tile.
Consec. Nos.
Consecutive numbers: for instance 3-4-5-6 in order. The note tells you whether you may choose where the run starts.
Like Numbers
The same number in different suits. A "like" group might be the same pair or kong repeated across the suits.
Matching Dragon
The dragon that pairs with a suit: Green with Bams, Red with Craks, White with Dots.
Opp. Dragon
Opposite dragon: a dragon that does not match the suit in question.
Any Dragon
Whichever dragon you like, as the note allows.
Kongs Match Pair
Wording like this means the pair and the kongs on the line must connect in the way the note describes. Read it as a rule about which numbers go together.
Dead hand
A hand that can no longer win, for example because of the wrong tile count or an error in exposing. A player with a dead hand keeps playing but cannot win.

How the card is organized

The card is divided into themed sections. A section tells you the theme the hands share, so you can scan quickly for the family of hands that fits what you were dealt. The sections do not rank hands by difficulty; use the printed value and the X or C mark for that.

Sections are the same year to year in spirit, but the specific hands change every year, so always play from the current card.

Joker rules

  • Where jokers work. A joker can stand in for any tile in a set of three or more identical tiles: a pung, kong, quint, or sextet.
  • Where they do not. Never in a pair and never as a single tile. A hand that is entirely pairs and singles, like those in the Singles and Pairs section, plays without jokers.
  • Flowers. Jokers can fill out a set of flowers of three or more, but not a pair of flowers.
  • No passing. A joker may not be passed in the Charleston.
  • Discarded jokers are dead. If someone discards a joker, nobody can call it or pick it up.
  • Exchanging. On your own turn, you may swap a natural tile from your rack for a joker sitting in any exposed set, yours or another player's, as long as your tile is the one the joker is standing in for. You take the joker and may use it in your own hand.
  • Calling with jokers. You may call a discard to complete a set that includes jokers from your rack, as long as the set is one that jokers are allowed in.
  • Jokerless bonus. A winning hand with no jokers in it earns a bonus (see the payments section).

Groups differ on small points. The National Mah Jongg League publishes the official rules, and your group's house rules take priority at your table.

The Charleston

The Charleston is the tile exchange before play begins. Each pass is three tiles, face down, and no jokers can ever be passed. Choose the tiles you want to be rid of before you pick up what you receive.

First Charleston (required)

  1. Pass right. Each player passes three tiles to the player on their right.
  2. Pass across. Three tiles go to the player opposite.
  3. Pass left. Three tiles go to the player on the left. A blind pass is allowed here: you may pass along tiles you just received without looking at them, using tiles from your rack to make up the rest.
  4. Vote. Everyone must agree to a second Charleston. If any one player says no, it stops.

Second Charleston (optional)

  1. Pass left. Three tiles to the player on the left.
  2. Pass across. Three tiles to the player opposite.
  3. Pass right. Three tiles to the player on the right. A blind pass is allowed on this last pass.

Courtesy pass

  1. Across, zero to three tiles. You and the player opposite each say how many tiles you want to swap, from none to three. If you name different numbers, the smaller number is the one you swap.

After the courtesy pass, East discards first and play begins.

A turn at the table

  • The deal. East takes 14 tiles; everyone else takes 13. Play goes counterclockwise.
  • On your turn. Draw one tile from the wall (or take a discard you are entitled to call), then discard one face up and say its name aloud.
  • Calling a discard. Any player may call the most recent discard to complete a pung, kong, quint, or sextet, or to win. You must expose that set face up on your rack. You cannot call a discard just to make a pair or a single, except to win.
  • Competing calls. A call for Mah Jongg beats any other call. Otherwise, the caller who would be next in turn order gets the tile.
  • Concealed hands. A hand marked C stays unexposed. The only discard you may take is the one that wins.
  • Winning. Call "Mah Jongg" when your 14 tiles match a hand on the card exactly, then show it. If the wall runs out first, the game is a draw and nobody is paid.

Payment calculator

Enter the point value printed on the hand you won. The calculator applies the common NMJL payment rules: the discarder pays double on a win off a discard, everyone pays double on a self-drawn win, and a jokerless hand doubles the value.

Hands that cannot contain jokers at all, such as those in Singles and Pairs, do not usually earn the jokerless bonus, so leave that box unchecked for them. Some groups add or drop bonuses, so check your table's house rules.
