You askedHow does the Fitbit compute the cardio load of an activity?

1How cardio load is computed

Cardio load is a version of TRIMP ("training impulse"), a sports-science measure from Banister. Every minute, your average heart rate is turned into a fraction of your heart rate reserve, the span between resting and maximum.

x = (HR − resting HR) ÷ (max HR − resting HR)x is the fraction of heart rate reserve (%HRR ÷ 100) for that minute
load per minute = 0.64 · x · ek·xk = 1.92 for males, 1.67 for females. The day's load is the sum over all minutes.

The exponential means harder minutes count disproportionately more. Google added three rules for all-day wear:

  • Below 30% HRR earns nothing.
  • Every scoring minute needs movement from the motion sensors, so stress alone doesn't count.
  • Minutes between 30% and 40% HRR are down-weighted. Google doesn't publish the factor.
Load earned per minute vs %HRR (k = 1.92). The shaded band is the reduced-credit zone; its exact factor isn't published, so the curve is dashed there.

This is why mowing the lawn counts: any minute with movement and enough heart rate scores, workout or not. Indoor cycling, walking and yard work all pass through the same formula. Confirming an activity type like "Maybe: Sport" improves the workout insights; the load comes from heart rate either way.

You askedHow does it set targets for me?

2Your weekly target

The app now sets a weekly target; daily load resets at midnight and adds toward it. The target estimates your typical ("chronic") load two ways and takes the larger:

target = max( RM , EWMA )RM: average weekly load over the past 28 days · EWMA: a smoothed average that changes slowly
  • After a break, the slow EWMA stops the target from collapsing. When you return, the faster rolling mean lets it recover.
  • A minimum target, calibrated to health guidelines, applies to everyone.
  • The first personal target appears after 7 days; the best estimate after 4 weeks.
  • With Premium and the Health Coach turned on, the Coach may adjust the target further.
Your screen: 322 of 230 = 140%. The ring turns green when the target is met and changes color again if you exceed it by a lot. Weeks well above target pull the 4-week average up, so the target rises gradually if you keep it up.
You askedHow is the maximum heart rate determined?

3Maximum heart rate

By default Fitbit doesn't measure it. It estimates it:

max HR = 220 − age = 220 − 75 = 145 bpmTanaka's formula, a better fit for older adults: 208 − 0.7 × 75 ≈ 155 bpm

220 − age is a population rule of thumb. Individuals commonly differ from it by 10 to 20 bpm either way, and it tends to run low for older adults. A max set too low inflates every minute's %HRR, and the exponential magnifies the error. Because your target is built from your own past load, a consistent bias largely cancels in the "% of target" figure.

You askedShouldn't a maximum require holding the heart rate for some duration?

5What counts as a max

Not holding it. Max heart rate is a ceiling you touch briefly at exhaustion. A rate you can sustain for several minutes is below your max. Physiologists look for a plateau: heart rate stops rising, by less than about 2–5 bpm per stage, even as the workload keeps increasing. Supporting signs:

  • You can't continue (about 17–19 on the 6–20 effort scale).
  • In a lab, a respiratory exchange ratio of about 1.10 or higher.
  • A value within about 10 bpm of the age-predicted max, as a sanity check.

Duration does matter for judging a sensor's reading: a one-second spike is suspect; a value that holds for several seconds, or repeats across hard sessions, is credible. At 75, a supervised exercise stress test is the safe way to measure a true max.

You askedShould I let Google set my max from my most recent workout?

6Accepting Google's suggested max

The logic is sound: a heart rate you really reached is a lower bound on your true max. Repeated prompts suggest 145 is too low for you. Check the workout's heart-rate graph first:

AcceptPeak climbs smoothly with effort, matches very hard breathing, and similar peaks repeat across sessions.
DeclineA sudden 20–30 bpm jump that vanishes within seconds, or a peak during comfortable effort.

Effect of accepting: each beat becomes a smaller share of your reserve, so load per minute falls at first. At a max of 155, the thresholds move from about 88 / 96 / 121 to about 91 / 100 / 128 bpm. Your target follows your own history, so it re-settles within a few weeks.

You saidI use a Polar H10 chest strap, and I can use it in place of the Charge 6 in the Google Health app.

7The Polar H10Your correction

The H10 measures electrically, like an ECG, so it isn't fooled by pedaling rhythm. I first relied on an older forum thread saying the Charge 6 couldn't use it; you corrected that. The Google Health app accepts it.

  • Max-HR prompts after H10 workouts are trustworthy. Ignore only a brief spike in the first few minutes, before the electrodes are moist. Wetting the strap first prevents it.
  • Workout load is more accurate during H10 sessions, such as indoor cycling.
  • Everything else still comes from the Charge 6: mowing, walks, daily activity and the overnight resting heart rate.

If Google keeps suggesting a higher max after H10 workouts, accept it.

You askedWhere in the app do I update the maximum heart rate?

8Where to change it

Google's help pages don't document a manual max-heart-rate setting in the Google Health app. They say zones are personalized and adjust over time, which matches the prompts you receive.

  • Zones are shown at Health tab → Heart → Heart rate. A manual edit, if one exists, is most likely there.
  • Also check Profile picture → Google Health settings. The older Fitbit app kept it under Heart Settings.
  • Ask the Coach: "How do I set a custom max heart rate?"
  • Simplest: accept the next suggestion that follows an H10 workout.
